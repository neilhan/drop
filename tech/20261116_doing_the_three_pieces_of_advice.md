# Doing the three pieces of advice

I wrote [three pieces of advice for new software engineers](20131208_3_pieces_of_advice_for_new_software_engineers.md)
in December 2013. Advice is cheap. Doing it is the whole job.
Thirteen years on, here is what doing each one actually looks like, in concrete steps.

# 1. Find a mentor — and be mentorable

- Pick one person you want to be as good as. Not the whole team, one person.
  If you want to be an architect, go to your architect.
- Ask for thirty minutes, and bring one specific question — not "teach me everything",
  but "how would you approach this problem I'm stuck on?"
- Do what they suggest, then go back and tell them what happened.
  That loop is what turns a conversation into mentorship.
- Be receptive. Almost every experienced person wants to help you grow,
  but it is hard to help someone who has already decided they are right.
  The value of you is how your team values you.
- When you are frustrated — micromanagement, ideas not valued — ask yourself
  before blaming the situation: what can I learn from this? What would benefit
  everybody involved? Everyone in your life is either a gift, or a lesson,
  and life will repeat a lesson until you get it.

# 2. Use externalized storage to help you think

- Get ideas out of your head and onto paper, or into an outliner, the same day.
  Your mind can only hold a few things at once; once it has a few, you stop exploring.
  The paper is the memory, your brain is the CPU.
- Capture in trees: ideas, todos, projects, information, each in its own branch.
  I use [workflowy.com](https://workflowy.com) for this. A mind map works too —
  Canadian schools teach kids a simple version of it, they call it a "web".
- Draw the connections. A couple of connected cycles on a page is often enough
  to get you thinking further again, over a much greater area than your head could cover alone.
- Review what you wrote, frequently. A note you never re-read is just storage, not thinking.
  A short weekly review beats a long monthly one.
- Read "Getting Things Done" by David Allen. Capture everything, clarify what it means,
  organize it, and then do it. It is the bible of productivity, and it only works
  if the system lives outside your head.

# 3. Read the "7 Habits" — then live one habit at a time

Stephen Covey's "The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People" is the book behind the advice.
The seven habits, in short:

1. Be proactive — you choose your response.
2. Begin with the end in mind — know what matters before you start.
3. Put first things first — do the important before the urgent.
4. Think win-win — look for the outcome that benefits everybody involved.
5. Seek first to understand, then to be understood.
6. Synergize — the team can find something better than any one person alone.
7. Sharpen the saw — keep renewing yourself, or the rest stops working.

- Do not try to live all seven at once. Pick one habit for a month and practice it
  on real work, then pick the next.
- Start with the second habit: write down your own answer to the ultimate question —
  your principle, your personal constitution, the purpose of your life.
  It is not an easy question, and it is the one that makes all the others effective.
  The earlier you find the answer, the more effective you can be.
- Re-read the book once a year. You will not read the same book twice —
  you will be a different person each time, and a different habit will speak to you.
